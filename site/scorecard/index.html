Ballot Question Scorecard

Six yes/no questions, asked of every Minnesota constitutional ballot question, the same way every time. The criteria freeze at public launch, before anything new is graded. Transparency scoring measures whether the question tells voters what the amendment does. It does not measure whether the amendment is good policy, and the calibration entries below prove the two are different things.

The six criteria

  1. Does the question quote the constitutional language the amendment deletes or modifies?Applies only when existing constitutional language is deleted or modified. A fail cites the deleted words and the question's silence.
  2. Does the ballot title name the thing being changed, rather than only a predicted benefit?Always applies. "Increasing funding to school districts" is a prediction; "Photo Identification Required for Voting" is a mechanism.
  3. If any control moves from the constitution to ordinary statute, does the question say so?Applies only when the amendment converts a constitutional command into something the legislature can later change by law.
  4. If the amendment gives anyone new authority or creates a new system, does the question identify it?Applies when new authority or machinery is created. The least mechanical criterion; close calls are flagged, not hidden.
  5. If new spending is authorized against a protected or dedicated fund, does the question disclose it?Applies only when the amendment opens a protected fund to a new category of spending.
  6. Does the question avoid asserting facts that would be true whether the amendment passes or fails?Always applies. A fact identical under yes and no carries no information about how to vote; its only function is implication. Test each clause: "still true if this fails?"

How scoring works

Frozen criteria. These six are v1.0; the freeze is stamped at public launch. Changes create a new version that applies only to questions scored after the change. No criterion is ever adjusted to reach a preferred result.

Every question, both directions. Every statewide constitutional ballot question gets scored, including ones we would vote for. When a question we support scores badly, the bad score gets published. That is the entire value of this page.

Documentary evidence. Every FAIL must cite verbatim text from the proposing law or the constitution. No fail stands on characterization alone. A quotation of a person carries a pin cite to the page it appears on.

Scores are not comparable across questions. Criteria that don't apply are marked N/A and excluded. The per-criterion verdicts are the substance; the ratio is a summary. The notable case is a question where every criterion applies and every criterion fails.

Entry #1 · The 2026 school fund amendment (HF 3900)

"Increasing funding to school districts." · Nov. 3, 20260 of 6
Question: "Shall the Minnesota Constitution be amended to increase the funding going to all school districts from the permanent school fund, which is a fund that supports school districts without raising individual income or property taxes, effective July 1, 2027?"
1FailDeletes 76 words of art. XI, §8, including "The principal of the permanent school fund shall be perpetual and inviolate forever." The question quotes none of it and does not mention that anything is deleted.
2FailTitle is a predicted benefit. It names no fund provision, no deletion, no mechanism.
3FailThe distribution formula moves from the constitution (net interest and dividends) to statute: "The distribution policy shall be prescribed by law." The question is silent.
4FailThe legislature gains rate-setting power over the new 4.5% payout (statutory, simple majority); administrative-cost payments run "in the manner prescribed by law." The question names no one.
5Fail"Reasonable and necessary administrative costs associated with managing the fund or school trust lands may be paid from the fund" — a new category of spending against the protected corpus, undisclosed.
6Fail"...without raising individual income or property taxes" is true today, true under yes, true under no. Invariant under the vote; pure implication.

Every criterion applies. Every criterion fails. This is the only question on this page with that distinction.

Calibration · four historical questions, same rubric

Scored retrospectively to demonstrate the rubric measures transparency, not politics. Sources: the proposing session laws, linked below.

Marriage amendment · 2012 (rejected by voters)2 of 2
Question: "Shall the Minnesota Constitution be amended to provide that only a union of one man and one woman shall be valid or recognized as a marriage in Minnesota?" Title: "Recognition of Marriage Solely Between One Man and One Woman"
1N/AAdded new language only; nothing deleted or modified.
2PassTitle names the mechanism exactly.
3N/ANo constitutional control moved to statute.
4N/ANo new authority or machinery created.
5N/ANo protected fund involved.
6PassThe question restates the operative text nearly verbatim; no invariant assertions.
Photo ID amendment · 2012 (rejected by voters)2 of 3
Question: "Shall the Minnesota Constitution be amended to require all voters to present valid photo identification to vote and to require the state to provide free identification to eligible voters, effective July 1, 2013?" Title: "Photo Identification Required for Voting."
1N/AAdded new language only; the quotation rule would not have applied (see below).
2PassTitle names the mechanism.
3N/ANo existing constitutional control moved to statute.
4FailThe full text created a provisional-ballot system and a "substantially equivalent identity and eligibility verification" standard for absentee voting, machinery the question never mentions. In dissent, Justice Alan Page — joined by Justice Paul H. Anderson — wrote that the question "constitutes a bait and switch" and that its words "were phrased to actively deceive and mislead" (LWV v. Ritchie, No. A12-0920, slip op. at D-12, D-26 (Minn. Aug. 27, 2012) (Page, J., dissenting)).
5N/ANo protected fund involved.
6PassBoth asserted requirements are effects of passage; nothing invariant.
Legacy Amendment · 2008 (adopted)1 of 2
Question: "...to dedicate funding to protect our drinking water sources ... by increasing the sales and use tax rate beginning July 1, 2009, by three-eighths of one percent on taxable sales until the year 2034?" Title: "Clean Water, Wildlife, Cultural Heritage, and Natural Areas"
1N/AAdded a new section only.
2FailThe title is all benefit and no mechanism: it does not mention a tax. The question, to its credit, states the rate and the end date plainly.
3N/ADedication moves control into the constitution, not out of it.
4N/AConstrains spending rather than granting new discretion. A close call, flagged per the criterion 4 note.
5N/ACreates funds; raids none.
6PassEvery clause describes an effect of passage: the tax, the rate, the dates, the dedications.
State lottery amendment · 1988 (adopted)3 of 5 · 1 pending
Question: "Shall the Minnesota Constitution be amended to permit the legislature to authorize a lottery operated by the state?"
1FailThis amendment modified existing text: art. XIII, §5's flat ban ("The legislature shall not authorize any lottery or the sale of lottery tickets") gained an exception. The question describes the change honestly ("permit" concedes a ban exists) but quotes nothing.
2PendingPending primary-source confirmation. Ballotpedia records no separate title beyond the question itself; if the 1988 sample ballot confirms that, this criterion scores on the question, which names the mechanism.
3PassThe shift is the disclosed point: "permit the legislature to authorize."
4PassThe new authority-holder is named in the question: the legislature.
5N/ANo protected fund involved.
6PassNo invariant assertions.

What calibration shows

Transparency is not policy agreement. The most controversial question on this page, the 2012 marriage amendment, scores perfectly: whatever you think of the policy, the question said exactly what the amendment did, and voters rejected it anyway. The beloved Legacy Amendment loses a point for a title that sells benefits and hides the tax. If this rubric were a partisan weapon, those two results would be reversed.

A known limit of the quotation rule. The 2012 photo ID amendment only added text, so a quote-what-you-delete rule would not have applied to it. Its deception was hidden additions (the provisional-ballot and absentee-verification machinery), which this scorecard catches at criterion 4 and which the proposed §204D.151 does not reach. Stated here so nobody discovers it as a gotcha.

Honest questions carry the rule at no cost. The 1988 lottery amendment modified an existing ban, so the rule would have required quoting one sentence. The 1988 question was honest, and the quotation would have cost it nothing. The rule burdens honest questions trivially and concealing ones fatally, which is the design working.
