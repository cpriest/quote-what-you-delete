What the amendment does

Minn. Const. art. XI, §8 struck = deleted · underlined = added
Deleted from the constitution: The principal of the permanent school fund shall be perpetual and inviolate forever. Deleted: …to secure the maximum return consistent with the maintenance of the perpetuity of the fund. Deleted: …all losses not offset by gains shall be repaid to the fund from the interest and dividends earned thereafter. Added: …and managed as a perpetual financial resource… Added: Reasonable and necessary administrative costs associated with managing the fund or school trust lands may be paid from the fund in the manner prescribed by law. Added: The distribution policy shall be prescribed by law…

"Perpetual" survives as a goal, not a rule. The struck text ordered losses repaid to the fund. The new text sets a management aim and leaves the rest to statute.

The payout rate isn't in the amendment. The new 4.5% distribution sits in ordinary statute (Minn. Stat. §11A.16, subd. 5, as amended). A future legislature can change it by simple majority.

What is happening

HF 3900 puts a constitutional amendment on Minnesota's November 2026 ballot. It rewrites the Permanent School Fund clause of the constitution: out goes the requirement that the fund's principal stay "perpetual and inviolate forever" and that investment losses be repaid from future earnings; in comes a total-return payout whose rate, definitions, and policy live in ordinary statute.

Voters won't learn any of that from the ballot. The question describes a benefit, increased school funding "without raising individual income or property taxes," and says nothing about what is being removed. The legislature wrote the title and question into the bill itself; under Limmer v. Ritchie (Minn. 2012) no one else may revise the title it chose, and under League of Women Voters v. Ritchie (Minn. 2012) courts defer to the question almost completely. In dissent, Justice Alan Page — joined by Justice Paul H. Anderson — wrote that the question "constitutes a bait and switch" and that its words "were phrased to actively deceive and mislead." The court let it through anyway.

There's a familiar name for this structure in the investment world: material omission. A prospectus made entirely of true sentences is still fraud if it leaves out what a reasonable buyer would need to know. Ballot questions face no such standard.

What changes hands

  • The payout rate (4.5% of the fund's trailing three-year average value) becomes a statute a future legislature can change by simple majority.
  • "Reasonable and necessary" administrative costs for managing the fund or school trust lands become payable from the fund, with the legislature defining reasonable.
  • The distribution policy as a whole moves from a self-executing constitutional formula to whatever the law says at the time.

The endowment model itself is mainstream, and income-only rules do distort how a fund invests. If the 4.5% rate belongs anywhere, it belongs in the constitution the amendment is already rewriting. The bill puts it in statute instead.

The proposed rule

Any ballot question proposing to amend the Minnesota Constitution that would delete or modify existing constitutional language must quote that language verbatim in the question as it appears on the ballot.

It is self-executing: either the deleted words appear in the question or they don't. No reviewing body, no budget, no discretion anywhere. It would have stopped this ballot question cold, and it burdens honest ones only trivially. The 2012 marriage amendment and the 2008 Legacy Amendment only added new language; the rule wouldn't have touched either. The 1988 lottery amendment modified an existing ban, so its question would have quoted one sentence — a cost an honest question carries easily.

The rule now exists as a bill ready for the 2027 session: a new section 204D.151 of the election code. Every compliant question gets the same fixed shape, the question, then a label line reading "This amendment would remove or change the following words of the Minnesota Constitution:", then the quotation, and nothing after it. The quoted words are exempt from any length limit, any future exemption must be enacted as a stand-alone section naming the rule it escapes, and any citizen can enforce compliance through the existing ballot-error statute.
